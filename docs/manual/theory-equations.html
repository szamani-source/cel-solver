Theory I: equations and numerics

The equations of motion of a solid written on a fixed mesh, and how celFoam solves them: an explicit Lagrange step with a Riemann solver at every face, followed by a remap of the moved material back onto the mesh.

Unknowns and conventions

Each cell holds the density \(\rho\), velocity \(\mathbf v\), Cauchy stress \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) (positive in tension), plastic history \(\mathbf h\) (plastic strain \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon^p\), equivalent plastic strain \(\bar\varepsilon^p\), and model variables such as the preconsolidation pressure \(p_c\)), and, for a free surface, the material volume fraction \(\alpha\); the rest of the cell is void. The pressure is \(p = -\tfrac13\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\sigma\). All quantities are cell averages (a cell-centred finite-volume method).

Governing equations

In the fixed (Eulerian) frame the material obeys conservation of mass and momentum and a rate form of the constitutive law Benson 1992:

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v) = 0,$$ $$\frac{\partial (\rho\mathbf v)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf v\otimes\mathbf v) = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \rho\,\mathbf g(t),$$ $$\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\sigma}{\partial t} + \mathbf v\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol\sigma = \mathbf W\boldsymbol\sigma - \boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf W + \mathcal{M}(\mathbf D;\,\boldsymbol\sigma,\mathbf h), \qquad \mathbf D = \operatorname{sym}\nabla\mathbf v,\quad \mathbf W = \operatorname{skew}\nabla\mathbf v .$$

\(\mathcal M\) is the material model (Theory II): an elastic response to the strain rate \(\mathbf D\), returned to the yield surface when the material yields. The terms with the spin \(\mathbf W\) make the stress rate objective (the Jaumann rate). The history and the volume fraction are carried with the material:

$$\frac{\partial (\rho\mathbf h)}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf h\otimes\mathbf v) = \rho\,\dot{\mathbf h}_{\text{model}}, \qquad \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\alpha\mathbf v) = \dot\alpha_{\text{void}} .$$

There is no energy equation: the models are isothermal and rate-independent (apart from the \(\mu(I)\) friction, which depends on the strain rate but not on temperature).

Operator split: Lagrange step plus remap

Each time step solves these equations in two parts, the classical Lagrange-plus-remap approach of hydrocodes and of Abaqus/Explicit CEL Benson 1992:

  1. Lagrange step. The cells are imagined to move with the material for one step \(\Delta t\). Only the material-derivative form is solved, with no convection: $$\frac{D\rho}{Dt} = -\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf v,\qquad \rho\frac{D\mathbf v}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \rho\mathbf g,\qquad \frac{D\boldsymbol\sigma}{Dt} = \mathbf W\boldsymbol\sigma - \boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf W + \mathcal M .$$
  2. Remap. The moved cells are mapped back onto the fixed mesh. This is pure transport of the conserved quantities: $$\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(q\,\mathbf v) = 0,\qquad q \in \{\rho,\ \rho\mathbf v,\ \rho\boldsymbol\sigma,\ \rho\mathbf h,\ \alpha\}.$$

The mesh never moves. The two steps share one face field, the Lagrangian volume flux \(\phi_{L,f} = \mathbf v^*_f\cdot\mathbf S_f\) (m³/s, positive out of the owner cell), where \(\mathbf v^*_f\) is the face velocity from the Riemann solver and \(\mathbf S_f\) the face area vector. Unlike a staggered (node-based) hydrocode, celFoam keeps every quantity at the cell centre and gets face values from a Riemann solver, in the spirit of Godunov methods for solids Trangenstein and Colella 1991 Miller and Colella 2001.

The Lagrange step

Face states

For each of the nine primitive components \(q\) (three of \(\mathbf v\), six of \(\boldsymbol\sigma\)) a Gauss gradient \(\nabla q\) is formed. On each side of a face the state is the cell value plus half a limited slope along \(\mathbf d\), the vector between the two cell centres, with \(a\) the jump across the face (the monotonised-central limiter of van Leer 1977):

$$q_{f} = q_P + \tfrac12\,\operatorname{minmod}\!\big(2a,\ \mathbf d\cdot\nabla q,\ 2(2\,\mathbf d\cdot\nabla q - a)\big).$$

A half-step (Hancock) predictor then advances the face states of each cell from its own limited states:

$$\mathbf v \leftarrow \mathbf v + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\rho}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \mathbf g\Big),\qquad \boldsymbol\sigma \leftarrow \boldsymbol\sigma + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\,\mathbb C:\operatorname{sym}\nabla\mathbf v ,$$

with the elastic stiffness \(\mathbb C\). This makes the scheme second order in space and time for smooth elastic waves (measured orders 1.9 to 2.3). Boundary faces reconstruct towards the boundary value through a mirror cell.

Gravity in equilibrium

A column at rest under gravity has a stress that grows linearly with depth. A plain reconstruction would see that gradient as a jump and set the column moving. celFoam takes each cell's hydrostatic jump across a face, \(\mathbf e = -\rho\,(\mathbf g\cdot\mathbf d)\,\hat{\mathbf g}\hat{\mathbf g}\), out of the slope and adds it back at the face, so a column in discrete equilibrium has identical face states on both sides and stays at rest to round-off (\(3\times10^{-17}\) m/s; decision 0030). This is a well-balanced scheme in the sense of the shallow-water literature LeVeque 1998. Gravity can be ramped in over a time \(T\) (gravityRamp):

$$\mathbf g(t) = \mathbf g\,\tfrac12\big(1-\cos(\pi t/T)\big),\qquad t<T .$$

Acoustic Riemann solver at each face

In the face-normal frame, with left and right states \((\mathbf v_L,\ \mathbf t_L = \boldsymbol\sigma_L\mathbf n)\) and \((\mathbf v_R,\ \mathbf t_R)\), and impedances \(z_p = \rho c_p\) for normal motion and \(z_s = \rho c_s\) for shear, the linearised (acoustic) Riemann problem of elastodynamics gives Toro 2009 Trangenstein and Colella 1991:

$$v_n^* = \frac{z_{pL}v_{nL} + z_{pR}v_{nR} + (t_{nR}-t_{nL})}{z_{pL}+z_{pR}},\qquad t_n^* = \frac{z_{pR}t_{nL} + z_{pL}t_{nR} + z_{pL}z_{pR}(v_{nR}-v_{nL})}{z_{pL}+z_{pR}},$$

and the same with \(z_s\) for the tangential velocity \(\mathbf v_t^*\) and shear traction \(\mathbf t_t^*\). Equivalently, each side lies on its own characteristic, \(\mathbf t^* = \mathbf t_L + z_L(\mathbf v^*-\mathbf v_L)\) and \(\mathbf t^* = \mathbf t_R - z_R(\mathbf v^*-\mathbf v_R)\), with wave speeds

$$c_p = \sqrt{\frac{M}{\rho}},\quad M = \frac{E(1-\nu)}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)},\qquad c_s = \sqrt{\frac{G}{\rho}},\quad G = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)} .$$

The terms \(z(v_R-v_L)\) are the jump terms. They make the scheme stable and remove odd-even modes, but they also act as a numerical viscosity of order \(\rho c h|\nabla\mathbf v|\) (\(h\) the cell size). For a stiff granular material in slow plastic flow this viscosity can exceed the real stresses, which is why the flow rules below exist.

Momentum, strain and stress

With face forces \(\mathbf F_f = \mathbf t^*_f|\mathbf S_f|\) and cell mass \(m = \rho V\):

$$m\,\Delta\mathbf v = \Delta t\Big(\sum_f \mathbf F_f + m\,\mathbf g\Big),\qquad \mathbf v \leftarrow \frac{\mathbf v}{1+\gamma\,\Delta t}\quad\text{(optional damping } \gamma\text{)}.$$

The velocity gradient comes from the face velocities by the Gauss theorem over the mid-step volume \(V_\text{mid} = \tfrac12(V+V_L)\):

$$\mathbf L = \frac{1}{V_\text{mid}}\sum_f \mathbf S_f\otimes\mathbf v^*_f,\qquad \Delta\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \Delta t\,\operatorname{sym}\mathbf L,\qquad \Delta\boldsymbol\omega = \Delta t\,\operatorname{skew}\mathbf L .$$

Stress and plastic strain are first rotated with the incremental rotation of (the Hughes-Winget rotation) Hughes and Winget 1980,

$$\mathbf Q = \big(\mathbf I - \tfrac12\Delta\boldsymbol\omega\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf I + \tfrac12\Delta\boldsymbol\omega\big),\qquad \tilde{\boldsymbol\sigma} = \mathbf Q\,\boldsymbol\sigma_n\,\mathbf Q^{\mathsf T},$$

then the material model is called once per cell: \((\boldsymbol\sigma_{n+1},\mathbf h_{n+1}) = \mathcal M(\tilde{\boldsymbol\sigma}, \Delta\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \mathbf h_n)\). Finally the moved volume and density are

$$V_L = V + \Delta t\sum_f \phi_{L,f},\qquad \rho_L = m/V_L .$$

The remap

The volume swept through face \(f\) in the step, \(\delta V_f = \Delta t\,\phi_{L,f}\), is taken from the donor cell (the cell it leaves). For every cell

$$m^{n+1} = m - \sum_{f} \rho_{\text{don}}(\mathbf x_s)\,\delta V_f,\qquad (m q)^{n+1} = m\,q_L - \sum_f \delta m_f\, q_{\text{don}}(\mathbf x_s),$$

summed with outflow positive, where \(\mathbf x_s\) is the centroid of the swept region and \(q\) any per-mass quantity (\(\mathbf v\), \(\boldsymbol\sigma\), \(\mathbf h\)). Density is reconstructed per volume, the others per mass, from a least-squares gradient with a limiter chosen per field (remapLimiters): monotonised central (MC) for \(\rho\), \(\mathbf v\), \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) and superbee Roe 1985 for the plastic strains, which keeps their sharp fronts. Mass, momentum and the volume of material are conserved to round-off (\(10^{-14}\) relative).

Material and void

With freeSurface yes each cell holds one material plus void, \(\alpha\in[0,1]\). celFoam uses its own face-by-face rules rather than a fluid VOF scheme, because a solid can carry shear, separate in tension and close void under compression (decisions 0021, 0031):

  • Slab position. A partly filled cell holds its material as a slab leaning, along each mesh direction, towards its fuller neighbour. Walls mirror the cell; open boundaries count as empty. With spreadLayers, a top layer whose material sits on its lower side spreads sideways instead.
  • Homogenised cells. Partly filled cells use the traction \(\alpha\boldsymbol\sigma\) and impedance \(\alpha z\), so their wave speed is the material's. They are first order. Empty cells do not move.
  • Contact and free faces. A face where material touches both sides gets the Riemann solution, unless the material has no tensile strength and the solution pulls. Every other face is traction-free, with the velocity of the side holding material. This is how cohesionless material separates and how the free surface forms.
  • Void closure. Compression closes void first; expansion unloads the material to zero normal stress, then opens void. Shear goes to the material.
  • Remap of \(\alpha\). The material volume crossing a face is the donor's material within the swept slice, from its slab position; mass follows at the donor's material density.
  • Small cells. Nearly empty cells and slivers next to walls are mixed with a neighbour so that their tiny mass does not limit stability (decisions 0092, 0094).

On wall-aligned meshes the slab and closure rules work along the mesh lines rather than along \(x, y, z\) (decisions 0104, 0106).

Flow rules for granular material

A frictional material with \(E\) of order 10 MPa flowing at 1 m/s has elastic wave speeds of about 100 m/s. The jump terms of the Riemann solver then act as a viscosity far larger than the granular stresses, and early runs showed a discharge rate that depended on \(E\), which a real granular flow does not (decisions 0034, 0036). The following options, all off by default and together called the T0 rule set (decision 0050), remove that dependence:

OptionRule
shearCapThe shear jump term at a contact face is scaled back by the largest factor \(\beta\in[0,1]\) that keeps the face stress within the Mohr-Coulomb surface. A capped face slips: each side keeps its own tangential velocity. One-cell shear bands can then form, as in granular material.
normalCapThe same cap on the normal jump term; the full cell strains with its own characteristic velocity at capped faces, keeping its volume.
flowImpedanceBetween flowing cells (stress within 3 % of the yield surface), the shear jump uses the tangent viscosity of the \(\mu(I)\) law, \(\eta = \partial\tau/\partial\dot\gamma\), in place of \(z_s\): each side contributes \(\min(z_s, 2\eta/\delta)\), \(\delta\) the distance between cell centres. The predictor drops the elastic stress rate along the plastic flow direction (decision 0040). With the nonlocal law the face viscosity comes from the nonlocal law's tangent (decision 0111).
spreadLayersPartly filled top cells spread sideways along the surface instead of leaning down (decisions 0024, 0032).
fullBlendCells with a void fraction between \(10^{-4}\) and \(10^{-2}\) blend the full-cell and partly filled rules with a weight falling from 1 to 0 in \(\log(\text{void})\), instead of switching (decision 0049).

With the T0 rule set the inclined-layer flow matches the Bagnold profile of the \(\mu(I)\) law within 5 %, the column collapse lies in the experimental band, and the discharge rate varies by about 20 % between \(E\) of 0.1 and 10 MPa (accepted for now). See Validation.

Boundary conditions

A boundary face uses a one-sided Riemann solution: the interior characteristic \(\mathbf t^* = \mathbf t_I + z_I(\mathbf v^* - \mathbf v_I)\) plus one condition from the boundary.

Frictional walls (decision 0030). Normal: \(v_n^* = 0\), giving \(t_n^* = t_{nI} - z_p v_{nI}\); if that is tension (\(t_n^* > 0\)) the material separates and the face is traction-free. Tangential: the sticking shear \(\mathbf t_t^\text{stick} = \mathbf t_{tI} - z_s\mathbf v_{tI}\) is allowed up to Coulomb's limit with the wall friction angle \(\delta_w\) and, on STL bodies, the adhesion \(a\),

$$|\mathbf t_t^*| \le a + \tan\delta_w\,|t_n^*| ;$$

beyond it the shear is clipped and the wall slips, with \(\mathbf v_t^*\) from the interior characteristic. On wedge meshes the wall stress is extrapolated to second order to keep the hoop balance at the wall.

Outlets (decision 0033). Before openTime a frictional wall. Afterwards a traction-free outflow face, \(\mathbf t^* = \mathbf 0\), \(\mathbf v^* = \mathbf v_I - \mathbf t_I/z_I\); the swept material leaves and is counted as discharged. If the outlet cell's material does not touch the face, the face still takes the free velocity so that void underneath passes out first.

Inlets (decision 0060). While open, the face moves into the domain at the given speed and the swept volume is filled with fresh material at the inlet density, stress \(-p_\text{in}\mathbf I\) and no plastic history.

Other patches. Symmetry planes (no normal velocity, no shear), prescribed velocity, traction-free, empty (2D), wedge, and cyclic/processor faces, which are solved once on the owner side and copied, so momentum crosses processor boundaries exactly.

Axisymmetric wedge

On a wedge mesh the two side faces carry the cell's own predicted velocity and traction, turned onto the face plane, with no volume flux and no Riemann jump. Together they supply the hoop strain rate \(u_r/r\) and the hoop force \(-\sigma_{\theta\theta}V/r\) of axisymmetric continuum mechanics (decision 0061). A thick cylinder under pressure matches Lamé's solution within 1 to 2 %.

Time step

The scheme is explicit, so the step is limited by the fastest wave crossing the smallest cell (the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition Courant et al. 1928):

$$\Delta t = \text{maxCo}\ \min_{\text{cells}}\frac{h}{c_p},\qquad c_p = \sqrt{\frac{M}{\rho_m}},$$

with the default \(\text{maxCo} = 0.4\) and \(\rho_m\) the material density of the cell. The length \(h\) is

  • \(V^{1/3}\) on Cartesian meshes (in 2D the cell thickness enters: give a 2D mesh the thickness of its cells, as the tutorials do);
  • the height between opposite faces, \(V\) over the largest face area, on angled meshes (decision 0103);
  • on a wedge, the smallest in-plane length combined with the cell radius \(r\) as \(1/h^2 = 1/h_\text{in}^2 + 1/r^2\), because the hoop strain adds a spring of length \(r\) (decision 0061).

For \(E = 10\) MPa, \(\nu = 0.3\) and \(\rho = 1800\) kg/m³, \(c_p \approx 87\) m/s, so 5 mm cells give \(\Delta t \approx 23\) µs. A softer material takes longer steps; this is why a soft material is a cheap way to test a setup.

Damping and settling

For settling only, the velocity is damped after each momentum update, \(\mathbf v\leftarrow\mathbf v/(1+\gamma\Delta t)\) with \(\gamma\) = damping (1/s), from dampingStart until the first outlet opens. With a gravity ramp of about 0.1 s and \(\gamma\) of 100 to 250 1/s, a silo comes to rest in a few tenths of a second. Apart from this, the only dissipation is numerical (the Riemann jump terms and the remap limiters) and the plastic work of the material.

One cycle

  1. Compute \(\Delta t\) from the CFL condition (shortened to land on write times); open or close outlets and inlets.
  2. Nonlocal fluidity (if used): solve its field equation with the stress of the step's start.
  3. Lagrange step: face states and predictor, Riemann solutions with walls, outlets, inlets, body surfaces and flow rules, momentum and damping, rotation and material model, \(V_L\), \(\rho_L\), \(\phi_L\).
  4. Move the bodies with their prescribed motion or with the force of the material on them (Theory III), and update the cells they cut.
  5. Remap \(\rho\), \(\mathbf v\), \(\boldsymbol\sigma\), \(\mathbf h\), \(\alpha\) onto the fixed mesh; count discharged and fed mass.
  6. Write fields, restart state and body output at write times.
